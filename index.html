01

What the simulator is for

It turns a hardware decision into a question with a measurable answer, before anyone buys a board or flies a field.

A scouting drone photographs a crop field and runs an AI model on board to find disease, such as leaf-blight lesions on maize. The model has to run on a small computer the drone carries, and there are several choices: a Raspberry Pi 5 alone, or with a Coral or Hailo-8 accelerator, or an FPGA board such as the KV260.

The usual way to choose is raw speed: the board that processes the most image tiles per second. On a drone that can mislead. Every board adds weight and draws power. The drone cannot fly faster than its camera or airframe allow, so extra processing speed beyond that point is wasted. Flying faster also blurs the photos, so more disease is missed.

The simulator follows the whole chain, from flight altitude to image resolution, processing workload, safe flying speed, detected disease and battery energy. It answers the question that matters: which board covers a hectare on the least energy while still finding enough of the disease?

Questions it answers

  1. Which board wins for a given crop, lesion size and detection target, and by how much.
  2. What limits the drone: the aircraft, the camera or the processor, at each altitude.
  3. Where the extra energy goes: flight time, added weight, the chip's own power, or flying speed.
  4. When the answer changes: which board is best for small vs large lesions, low vs high flight.
  5. What to measure first: which inputs move the result most, so lab time goes where it counts.

What it is not: a replacement for field trials. It compares options fairly under identical conditions and shows how the answer depends on each input. Absolute numbers become trustworthy as measured values replace the estimates, and one real flight checks the prediction.

05

How accurate it is

Accuracy has two parts: whether the simulator computes correctly, and whether its inputs match reality. The first is checked; the second improves with each measurement.

Checked

The maths is right

  • The full flight simulation agrees with the closed-form model: recall within ~2 points, energy within 1%, for all four boards.
  • The Python version used for Gazebo matches the browser exactly on 200 random configurations.
  • The Gazebo pipeline passes a self-test with a hidden known answer.
  • Survey geometry, tiles and the speed limit are exact arithmetic.
Only as good as its inputs

The numbers

  • Board speed and power are estimates today. They decide most of the result.
  • Detection uses a placeholder curve until our model is tested on held-out Cornell images.
  • Drone power uses textbook rotor physics, not yet calibrated with a measured hover.
  • Every input is labelled, and the counter above shows how many are measured.
Not yet validated

Against reality

  • No real flight has been compared with a prediction yet.
  • The test field is a synthetic layout; its effect on the board ranking still has to be shown to be small.
  • The Gazebo camera model has not been checked against real Pi photos.

In short: the simulator is reliable for comparing boards and for showing how the answer depends on each input. Absolute figures, such as Wh per hectare or field recall, are estimates until the measurements below are done.

06

Gazebo confirmation

The fast simulator simplifies flight and imaging. Gazebo checks whether those simplifications change which board wins.

What Gazebo adds

  • The PX4 autopilot flying the survey: acceleration, turns, wind, altitude-hold error
  • Rendered camera frames with lens blur, noise, rolling shutter and exposure
  • The real detector on those frames, or the real board over the network
  • 3D maize with lesions cut from real held-out Cornell drone photos

Same field, both tools

Any field in the simulator can be exported and rebuilt in Gazebo, lesion for lesion. A Python port of the simulator's maths matches the browser exactly, so both tools start from identical inputs and the results can be compared directly.
